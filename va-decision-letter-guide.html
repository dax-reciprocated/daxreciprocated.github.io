How to Read a VA Disability Decision Letter

Denied? Granted a percentage that doesn't make sense? Before you throw another stack of paperwork at VA, learn what the decision actually says. This free guide walks through the important parts in plain English.

By DAX Reciprocated LLC • Veteran-led educational publishing • Updated November 2026 • Approximately 7-minute read

Start here.

Don't stop reading at GRANTED, DENIED, or the percentage. A decision notice and its enclosures can explain what VA decided, the evidence it considered, favorable findings, reasons for denial or evaluation, and review options. Layout varies by decision type and date.

1. Get the complete decision letter

Start with the entire notice, including any attached rating decision and review-rights information. You can generally access available decision letters through VA.gov's claim and appeal status tool after signing in. VA may also mail you a copy.

Don't confuse a Benefit Summary Letter—often used to prove benefit status—with the detailed decision notice explaining why VA decided an issue. They serve different purposes.

Make sure the letter is complete, readable, and dated. Keep a secure copy; don't post an unredacted decision letter publicly.

2. Find the issues VA actually decided

A single decision can cover several conditions, and each can have a different result. Look for the section identifying the issues or decisions. Ask:

  • Which claimed condition or benefit did VA address?
  • Was service connection granted or denied?
  • If granted, what evaluation and effective date were assigned?
  • Was an issue deferred for additional development instead of finally decided?

A grant of service connection, the evaluation percentage, and the effective date are separate questions. Avoid treating one favorable finding as proof that everything else in the decision is correct.

3. Read the Evidence section like an inventory

The Evidence section identifies records VA says it considered. Depending on the case, it may mention service treatment records, VA or private medical records, lay statements, examinations, or earlier decisions.

Claim Autopsy™ rule: establish the baseline.

Write down the document name and date. Compare the list with the records you expected VA to have. If something looks absent, that's a question to investigate—not automatic proof that VA ignored it.

A document appearing in an evidence list doesn't, by itself, tell you how VA weighed it. That's why the reasons and explanation sections matter too.

4. Understand the Reasons for Decision

This is where you stop guessing. Read the explanation for each issue, not just the first page or the final combined rating.

If service connection was denied

Look for what VA says wasn't established. The missing element may involve the existence of a qualifying disability, an in-service event or exposure, the relationship to service, or another legal requirement. Different claim types—including presumptive and secondary claims—can have different evidence requirements. Do not assume that every denial means “get a nexus letter.”

If service connection was granted

Read the assigned percentage and the criteria discussed for that evaluation. A grant does not automatically mean the Veteran agrees with the percentage or the effective date. Compare what VA explains with the applicable rating criteria and facts in the decision.

Look for favorable findings

Favorable findings identify elements VA has already found in your favor. They matter when understanding what remains disputed. Under 38 CFR § 3.104(c) , qualifying favorable findings generally bind later adjudicators unless rebutted under the rule's clear-and-unmistakable-error standard.

5. Check the dates, ratings, and payment details

Pay attention to the decision date, assigned evaluation, effective date, combined evaluation, and any stated monthly payment amount or start date. These aren't necessarily the same dates or numbers.

Your combined rating is generally not the arithmetic sum of individual disability percentages. VA applies its combined-ratings rules, and the bilateral factor may apply in eligible situations.

6. A quick Claim Autopsy™ example: Sergeant Harry Sachs

Meet Sergeant Harry Sachs, a fictional Veteran used for training examples in The Exotic Veteran™ Field Manual. Suppose Harry receives a letter showing that one condition was granted and another was denied. He immediately declares the whole decision wrong.

Instead of relying on that first reaction, Harry should separate the issues and examine what the notice says about each one:

Issue A — Granted What evaluation and effective date did VA assign? Which criteria did its explanation discuss?
Issue B — Denied Which required element does VA say was not met? What evidence and favorable findings did it identify?

The point isn't that VA always gets it right—or that Harry always does. It's that the written decision provides a factual starting point. That's the heart of the Claim Autopsy™: examine before you react.

7. The five-question Claim Autopsy™ checklist

  1. Did I read the entire notice and all relevant enclosures?
  2. Can I identify every issue VA decided and its result?
  3. Do I know which evidence VA says it considered?
  4. Can I explain the stated reasons and favorable findings in my own words?
  5. Have I checked the decision date, review rights, and applicable deadlines?

8. If you disagree, don't ignore the deadline

VA generally offers three decision-review paths for qualifying disability compensation decisions: a Supplemental Claim, Higher-Level Review, or Board Appeal. The rules about new evidence and deadlines differ.

For many decisions, Higher-Level Review and Board Appeal requests must be made within one year of the decision-letter date. A Supplemental Claim can generally be filed later, but filing within a year can be important for potential effective-date protection. Some types of decisions have shorter deadlines or special rules. Read your specific notice.

This guide doesn't tell you which review option to choose. For individualized assistance with a claim, seek a VA-accredited representative . Accredited VSO representation is free.

Common questions

Can I download my VA decision letter online?

For many claim and decision types, yes. Sign in at VA.gov and use the claim or appeal status tool to access available decision letters.

Does a favorable finding mean the entire claim should be granted?

No. A favorable finding may establish one element while other elements or evaluation questions remain unresolved.

Does every denial require a medical nexus letter?

No. The evidence needed depends on the claim type and the reason for the denial, including possible presumptions and other rules.
